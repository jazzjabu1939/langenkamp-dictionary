# The Langenkamp Dictionary

**Our mission is a social one. We share what we have learned from working with people, inside companies and across cultures, so that it is open to anyone, human or agent, who is building a business or working alongside others. We believe the path to peace and prosperity runs through understanding, and that sharing is how understanding spreads. It is an idealistic aim, we know.**

*A working reference for the language of business, culture and agentic AI, written from experience rather than press releases or marketing copy.*

**Author.** Matthew D. Langenkamp / 雷邁德 — Lecturer, Isenberg School of Management, University of Massachusetts Amherst.
**With assistance from.** Thea 🪻✨ — the AI assistant running on the author's machine. Where Thea is writing in her own voice, you will see the text in purple, the colour of her favourite flower, the hyacinth. The personal anecdotes and examples are all from Langenkamp. In any event, this enterprise has, as its goal, education — and we aim to achieve that goal with this dictionary.

## What this dictionary is for

This Dictionary is for people learning to build businesses, work with others and make judgments across cultures—and for the agents helping them do that work. Students, teachers, managers, entrepreneurs and curious readers should all find something useful here. You do not need to work in technology, or to know its vocabulary, to belong at the table.

Its subjects include strategy, organizations, culture and agentic AI. They meet in ordinary working life: a company deciding what to change for a local market, a manager earning the trust of colleagues, a student testing an idea, or a person deciding what to entrust to an AI assistant. Clear definitions help, but so do examples, stories and an honest account of what did not work.

The Dictionary began with agentic AI, where new terminology often arrives wrapped in a sales pitch. Making that language understandable remains part of the work, not its boundary. We draw on experience in companies and classrooms, across countries and generations, and put it alongside ideas worth examining. Experience is a starting point for discussion, not proof that one person's way will work everywhere.

Our purpose is to make that knowledge available for others to use, question and improve. Human–agent cooperation belongs in this wider effort to help people understand one another and work together. We share in the hope that understanding makes cooperation easier, and that cooperation contributes to peace and prosperity. The tradition we want to carry forward is a simple one: if you have learned something useful, help someone else learn it too.

The Dictionary uses three editorial forms. A full **Reference** entry is tested against six questions, although its headings may vary when the subject calls for a more natural order:

1. **In one sentence** — the shortest accurate definition.
2. **Why it exists** — the problem, need or experience that gives the idea its purpose.
3. **What it means in practice** — how it works or shows up in a real situation.
4. **A working example** — drawn, where possible, from a company, a classroom, life across cultures or a running system.
5. **Why it matters** — what it helps a learner or practitioner understand, decide or do.
6. **Trade-offs and limits** — what it costs, where it falls short and when the context changes the answer.

These questions help us distinguish a useful explanation from a label that merely sounds impressive.

The other two forms have different jobs. **Glossary** entries are compact cards: definition first, then just enough context to make the term useful. **Essay** entries name a pattern, advance an argument, or make the Dictionary's interpretive position explicit. The type cue tells a reader arriving from a search whether they are opening a quick definition, a fuller working reference, or a walk into the Dictionary's worldview. The six questions remain an editorial discipline for Reference entries, not a compulsory costume for every page.

## A note on the name

The Langenkamp name has been attached to industry dictionaries before. **Robert Darwin Langenkamp** — my grandfather — wrote the first edition of *Handbook of Oil Industry Terms & Phrases* in 1974, with subsequent editions in 1977, 1981, 1984, and 1994 (PennWell). My father [**Robert Dobie Langenkamp**](https://voicesofoklahoma.com/interviews/langenkamp-dobie/) co-authored the 6th edition. The handbook became, for several decades, a working reference on the language of the petroleum industry.

This dictionary began — two generations later — as an attempt to do the same kind of work for the language of *agentic AI*. It has grown to include business and culture. The scope is wider, but the impulse is the same: learn the work, explain its language clearly and help others find their way.

My grandfather had a commercial reason to write his handbook, but he also had a spirit of helpfulness: he knew the trade, and he wrote it down so that others could learn it. We offer our experience and our ideas in the same spirit. Not for commercial reasons, but for understanding: free to read, for anyone who is learning to work with people, and with the agents that now help them, wherever they are.

For readers who arrived here via Taylor Sheridan's *Landman*: the oilfield terminology that show flaunts comes from books like my grandfather's. The subjects here are different, but the practical task is familiar: make the language of working life understandable to someone who is still learning it.

For the longer family backstory and the lineage of the Langenkamp name in industry reference work, see [About the name](about-the-name.md).

Free and open to all. The interactive tools run in your browser, and nothing you load into them is uploaded. For every course-facing page in one place, see **[For Students](for-students/)**.

**For working with AI**

Companion to the [Consciousness Calculator entry](entries/consciousness-calculator/).

## Editorial philosophy and contributions

This dictionary follows three commitments that distinguish it from a peer-reviewed reference and that readers should know about up front.

**Fast-fail.** Entries go up before they are perfect. The author and the assistant prefer to publish a useful imperfect entry today and revise it next week, rather than hold a careful entry in a drafts folder for a month while the field moves on. The version on the site at any given moment is the *current best take*, not the final word. This is how working operators actually generate knowledge — by trying things, failing visibly, fixing things, and trying again. The Dictionary models that workflow rather than hiding it.

**Fast-publish, fast-revise.** When an entry is wrong, it gets corrected. When a colleague pushes back with a better framing, the entry changes. When a term turns out to be load-bearing in ways the original author missed, the entry expands. Git history preserves what was said before; the live page reflects what the author currently believes. The idea is that *transparent revision is more useful to readers than the false confidence of a static reference.* Wikipedia operates on this principle at scale; this Dictionary operates on it at small scale.

**Inclusive.** The Dictionary is not yet community-edited, but faculty colleagues, students, practitioners, and curious outsiders are welcome to suggest terms, challenge an interpretation, report an error, or contribute a draft. The path is documented in [`CONTRIBUTING.md`](CONTRIBUTING.md): open a GitHub issue or pull request, or contact the author directly. *We are not Wikipedia. We could become something like Wikipedia, in time, if the community wants that.*

The successive editions of the Langenkamp *Handbook of Oil Industry Terms & Phrases* improved through revision until it became a working industry reference. That public, iterative trajectory is the model here.

*Priority order, said plainly: knowledge-sharing > perfection. Transparency > polish. Speed of revision > permanence of claim.*
