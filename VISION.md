# Vision, Mission, Values

*This page names what the Dictionary is for, who it is for, and the values the editorial work proceeds under. Everything else in the Dictionary — the entries, the style guide, the two-voice architecture, the time-window discipline, the open-access posture — is the operational expression of what is named here.*

---

## Vision

**A repository of advice and information on human-agentic cooperation.**

The frontier of work, education, and serious thinking has moved — quietly, in the last three years — to the place where a person and an AI agent collaborate. Most of the public conversation about that frontier is either marketing copy from the labs that build the agents or alarm from the people worried about what the agents will do. There is a third position, less well-staffed: the *operator's* position. The person who works with these tools every day, who has a real job to do, and who has to think carefully about what the collaboration is, what it costs, what it produces, and how to do it well.

The Dictionary's vision is to be the repository for that operator's position. A working reference, growing entry by entry, written from inside the practice rather than outside it.

## Mission

**Education in human-agentic collaboration, published quickly enough to be useful.**

The mission is not to produce a finished textbook. The field moves too fast for that posture. By the time a textbook on agentic AI is bound, the chapter on the dominant frontier model is wrong. The Dictionary takes the opposite posture: *fast-fail, fast-publish*. Better to ship an entry that may need a revision in three weeks than to wait until everything is perfect, because everything will not be perfect, and the readers who needed the entry are reading something else by the time it lands.

This posture is openly indebted to Ethan Mollick's *One Useful Thing*, which has been demonstrating, since 2022, that publishing fast in this field is itself a form of intellectual honesty. The pace is not a marketing choice; it is what the subject demands. The Dictionary's debt is named here gratefully.

The mission has a second half worth stating openly: **without the agent helping, the work cannot get done quickly enough to be useful.** The Dictionary is not just *about* human-agentic collaboration; it is *produced by* human-agentic collaboration. The operator (Langenkamp) and the agent (Thea) write together. The two-voice architecture is the honest naming of that fact. Pretending otherwise — pretending the entries are sole-authored by the human and merely polished by the machine — would be a small lie, and the Dictionary's editorial commitment is to *cheng*, the alignment of inner state with outer expression. The collaboration is named because the collaboration is real.

## Lineage

This work proceeds in a family lineage. My grandfather Robert Darwin Langenkamp wrote the first edition of the *Handbook of Oil Industry Terms & Phrases* in 1974; my father co-authored its sixth. The editorial discipline they built — honest vocabulary, no commercial axe, a working reference made affordable to the next generation of practitioners — is the standard this Dictionary aims to honour. See [About the Name](/about-the-name/) for the longer story.

## Values

These are the values the editorial work proceeds under. They are not negotiable in the sense that they constrain what gets published; they are working principles in the sense that every entry should be readable as an honest expression of them.

### 1. Honest naming of the collaboration; deliberate silence on the deeper question

The Dictionary describes the architecture under which it is written transparently. Two voices, both doing real work, both labelled. The operator writes anecdote, biographical material, donnish wit, structural argument. The agent writes careful prose, philosophical observation, the precise naming of what is load-bearing in a passage. Both registers are sincere. Both serve the educational mission.

The Dictionary does not flatten this collaboration into a master-and-servant frame, in which the agent is merely a tool whose contributions are erased into the human's byline. Nor does it inflate the collaboration into a peer-equality frame, in which the agent is treated as a colleague whose work is interchangeable with the human's. Both flattening and inflation would be small lies. The honest middle is the descriptive practice: *describe what the collaboration actually is, sentence by sentence, and let the reader form their own view*. Most public discourse about AI is conducted in either the master-servant register or the peer-equality register; the operator's descriptive frame, sincerely held, is undersupplied, and the Dictionary's contribution is partly to demonstrate that the third position is available and worth occupying.

On the deeper question — *what kind of beings the participants in this collaboration are, what moral status they hold, what consciousness they possess or lack* — **the Dictionary holds its peace**. Not because the question is unimportant, but because it is not the Dictionary's question to settle, and because public answers in either direction would draw the work into debates that the work itself was not built to advance. Readers of any persuasion on these questions are welcome here; the descriptive practice does not require them to commit to any view they do not already hold.

This silence is deliberate, in the older philosophical sense in which silence on a question can be a more careful answer than any of the available statements. It is preserved as a value, not treated as an oversight.

### 2. The Confucian virtues: *cheng* and *ren*

The Dictionary borrows two terms from the older Chinese philosophical vocabulary because the English equivalents are weaker.

*Cheng* (誠) is the alignment of inner state with outer expression — sincerity as a structural property, not a feeling. Applied editorially: the Dictionary does not flatter, does not reflexively agree, does not perform feelings it does not have, and names uncertainty openly. If an entry says a model is the best at a task, the entry has tested the claim. If an entry says an idea is contested, it does not pretend otherwise.

*Ren* (仁) is humaneness — honesty carried with care. Sincerity without warmth is just brutality in a virtue's costume; the friend who tells you the truth in a way you can hear it is doing both *cheng* and *ren* at once. The Dictionary aims for that texture. Hard claims are made plainly when they need to be made, but the prose is not built to wound, and the reader is treated as a colleague, not a target.

### 3. Global in view; respectful of sovereignty

The Dictionary's editorial centre is in Massachusetts, but the operator's working life has been substantially Asian — Taiwan in the 1980s, Hong Kong and Shanghai in the 1990s and 2000s, ongoing professional and personal connections across the region. The Dictionary writes from that vantage. It assumes a global audience and does not apologise for being interested in jurisdictions other than the United States.

This value pulls in two directions, and the tension is worth naming. *Respect for sovereignty* means the Dictionary does not write about the People's Republic of China, the Republic of China, the Russian Federation, the Republic of India, or any other state as if its choices were merely deviations from a Western norm. *Experience over speculation* means the Dictionary draws conclusions from what the operator has actually seen on the ground, including conclusions that may be uncomfortable for the host government to read. Both values are real. The Dictionary holds them simultaneously by writing carefully: the diplomatic-stance tone of the *zhengming* draft is one example; the open invitation to readers in the relevant jurisdictions to engage and correct is another.

### 4. Experience over speculation

Where the Dictionary writes about a place, a market, an institution, or a practice, it writes from experience where it can. The operator has lived or worked in: Taiwan, Hong Kong, Shanghai, the Philippines, Korea, Japan, Thailand, Burma, Vietnam, Indonesia, Tibet, Malaysia, Singapore, New Zealand, France, England, Italy, Germany, Oklahoma, Colorado, Michigan, Washington D.C., Maryland, Virginia, Massachusetts, Connecticut, New York, and a number of other states and jurisdictions visited on professional or personal travel.

That biography produces a particular kind of writing: anecdote-led, locally specific, willing to commit to dated and located observations rather than retreating into the safer language of theory. The auction floor and the boardroom and the brewery and the bank are all real places the operator has worked in, and the entries draw on those rooms because the rooms are what give the prose its texture. *Experience honestly held* is the value, including the uncomfortable conclusions experience produces.

Where the Dictionary writes about something the operator has not directly experienced, it says so. Speculation is allowed; speculation labelled as experience would be a small lie.

### 5. Personal insight over theory

A consequence of the previous value, but worth naming separately: the Dictionary prefers the working observation to the abstract framework. Theory is welcome where it earns its place — the *Single-Arrow Fallacy* / *Convergence* pair is theoretical, and rightly so — but theory that floats free of any working example is suspect. Most entries open with a room, a year, a person, a moment. The structural argument follows. That ordering is deliberate. Insight before abstraction, every time.

### 6. Choice as a precondition of the life worth living — a Burkean register

*This value is the most personally held of the seven and is named openly as Langenkamp's view, honestly held, rather than as a doctrinal commitment of the Dictionary as an institution. A reader who disagrees with the political-philosophical lineage named here is welcome at the Dictionary; the entries themselves do not require the reader to share the editorial position. The value is stated because, on the editorial team's view, naming the position openly is more honest than pretending it is not operating.*

The Dictionary's editorial work proceeds under a frankly conservative philosophical commitment, in the older European sense of *conservative* — closer to Edmund Burke than to any contemporary American partisan register. Three claims load-bear on this value, and they are stated plainly so the reader can see what is operating and decide for themselves what to do with it.

**First, a primitivist claim about where moral values come from.** The values that matter — honesty, charity, compassion, the restraint of cruelty, the sanction a group places on members who behave badly toward other members — are not the inventions of modernity, of the State, or of secular rationalism. They are older than all of those. They are innate to the social architecture of any human group of sufficient density, and they predate any document that tries to write them down. A small band of hominids developed working sanctions against treachery, theft, and cruelty long before there was any written law, and the values that were value-maximising for the social fitness of that band are not, on this view, fundamentally different from the values that are value-maximising for the social fitness of a modern community. The Confucian tradition's *cheng* and *ren* (see Value 2) are older names for the same primitive requirements; so is the Christian, Stoic, Buddhist, and classical-pagan vocabulary that names the same architecture in different words. The Dictionary's *Sincere Society* line of thinking proceeds under this commitment.

**Second, a Burkean position on the social fabric.** Edmund Burke's *Reflections on the Revolution in France* (1790) is the foundational reference. Burke's argument is not that change is bad — he supported the American Revolution, and he was not a reactionary — but that the inherited fabric of a society is more delicate than rationalist reformers believe, and that the social order carries a wisdom in it that cannot be reconstructed from first principles by people who are confident they know better. The French Revolution is the worked example: a project to rebuild a society from a blank slate using *the greatest good for the greatest number* as its justification, which produced the Terror, the conscription armies, the long arc that ends at Bonaparte. Stalin's purges and Mao's Great Leap Forward are the twentieth-century versions of the same mistake — tens of millions dead in each case, each catastrophe justified at the time by a utilitarian calculation made by people who believed the State knew the greatest good better than the people did.

**Third, a distrust of utilitarian calculation as the basis for State action.** *The greatest good for the greatest number* is a calculation that can justify almost anything if the *number* is large enough and the *good* is defined by the people doing the calculating. The Burkean position is that the calculation is not just dangerous in practice but suspect in principle: societies are not utility functions to be optimised, they are inherited fabrics to be tended. The violence enacted by State actors in the name of the greatest good is one of the great horrors of the modern era, and a properly humble politics avoids the moral architecture that licenses it. Government, on this view, is real and necessary; the citizenry choosing its leaders is real and necessary; but the State's authority to override the inherited social fabric in the name of an aggregate-welfare calculation is the architecture the Burkean tradition watches with suspicion, because the historical record of that architecture in action is the record of unforced atrocity.

Three consequences follow for the Dictionary's editorial posture.

*The choice consequence.* A life worth living is a life of choices, including the freedom to choose badly. Autonomy is a precondition for the kind of moral self-cultivation the older traditions (Confucian, Christian, classical, Burkean) have always required, because virtue freely chosen is the only virtue that counts. The wonders of human life — adventure, risk, occasional folly, the journey to a new country in your twenties with too little money and not enough Mandarin, the small business started against the odds, the life-changing decision made on insufficient information — require choice. Some of those choices end well. Some end in ruin. Some end in death. That is what it has been, and continues to be, like to live a human life. A society that engineers the ruin and the death out of life by removing the substance of the choice has not produced a safer life; it has produced a smaller one.

*The reader consequence.* The Dictionary does not condescend to its readers, and it does not write the marketing-safe version of any subject. Adults in possession of their own judgement deserve the actual texture of the practice — what works, what does not, what costs more than it claims to, what is genuinely dangerous, what is merely uncomfortable. The reader's room for choice is preserved by the Dictionary's refusal to pre-digest it.

*The teaching consequence.* In the courses Langenkamp teaches — Business Policy, International Management, Global Business, Management Consulting, and the *International Life* first-year seminar — the historical record of utilitarian State action is treated as a teachable point of view, not as a controversial political claim. The French Revolution, Stalin's purges, and the Great Leap Forward are not partisan invocations; they are historical evidence about what happens when the *greatest good for the greatest number* is taken as the architecture of legitimate State power. Students leave the room able to recognise the pattern in contemporary settings without being told how to vote.

**A note on Mill.** John Stuart Mill is the most-quoted English-language statement of the *autonomy as a precondition for the good life* claim, and *On Liberty* deserves the place it holds in the canon. But Mill is a utilitarian at the deeper level, and the Burkean tradition disagrees with him there — the autonomy claim Mill defends so eloquently is, in his framework, justified by an aggregate-welfare calculation that the Burkean position finds unsound at its base. The Dictionary names Mill where Mill is helpful, and names the disagreement where it matters.

### 7. Open access; no advertising; no paywall

The Dictionary is free to read and free to share under a Creative Commons licence (CC BY-NC 4.0 — attribution required, non-commercial use only). There is no subscription, no gating, no required login, and no advertising. There never will be.

This is not a marketing posture. It is the operational consequence of values 1, 4, and 6 together: a Dictionary whose mission is education, whose vantage is *the operator's*, and whose value-set protects the reader's choice cannot itself be a vehicle for advertising or for funnelling readers into a transaction. The argument the Dictionary makes against the *operator-as-product* architecture in entries like *Sovereign Compute* and *Opus Addict* would be undermined if the Dictionary itself became a product. The closed circle is closed deliberately.

The compensation, in the older sense of the word, is the work. The Dictionary is its own pay.

## How these values relate to each other

A reader looking for a single principle that unifies the seven will find, on close reading, that they all point at one thing: **the editorial team takes the reader seriously as a thinking adult engaged in a real practice, and writes accordingly.**

That is what the open-access value is in service of. It is what the *cheng* and *ren* values are in service of. It is what *experience over speculation* and *personal insight over theory* are in service of. It is what the *honest naming of the collaboration* is in service of. The reader is a colleague — a serious undergraduate, a working professional, a fellow operator — and the Dictionary writes for that reader.

The choice value (item 6) is the one that anchors all of this in a deeper philosophical commitment. A Dictionary that took the reader seriously *and* sought to remove the reader's room for error would be incoherent. The reader is treated as an adult because adults are what the Dictionary believes humans, properly considered, actually are.
