## Executive Summary

Dartmouth’s provost resigned after a faculty committee found that he had not disclosed his use of AI in published writing. The committee relied on his university AI account logs and publishers’ policies rather than on AI detectors. At SUNY Geneseo, the head of an AI tutoring company emailed students about their professors’ AI rules, and the provost replied that instructors decide. Faculty at Princeton, Stanford and Berkeley are moving graded work into the classroom. In our own tests, an AI agent played the Capstone 2.0 simulation and scored well above our midterm threshold, so an unsupervised Capstone score no longer shows on its own who made the decisions. Shorter items cover AI policy work at Florida A&M, Oregon and College of DuPage, new Claude licenses at Georgetown and Emory, and AACSB’s new survey of deans.

## Capstone 2.0: An AI Agent Can Play the Simulation

Capstone 2.0 is the Capsim business simulation used in our 494BI sections. In the Footrace, each student runs a company against computer-run rivals for a score out of 600. Unsupervised Capstone work has never been fully secure: students could hire online “tutors” or work with friends, so proctored exams were already prudent. AI now makes it easy to have the company operated for you. The risk is greatest in the individual Footrace and smaller in Tournament play between student teams.

On October 18–19, an AI agent working in a web browser played our practice instance, entering every decision across eight four-year trials. Its official scores ranged from 506.0 to 551.3; our midterm required 400. [16] The trials show that the work can be delegated, not that any student delegated it.

At the October 26 proctored midterm, some students who had earlier submitted screenshots of scores above 400 did not reach that level again, and some of those screenshots showed open AI-tool tabs with “Capstone” in the prompts. [17] Exam-day scores can fall for ordinary reasons, and an open tab does not show what a tool did. The observation concerns the assessment, not individual students.

Footrace exams are proctored in person. We are also testing browser lockdown and browser monitoring options, though software cannot stop an agent running on a second device.

## Dartmouth: Records Settled the Question

Dartmouth Provost Santiago Schnell resigned on October 28 after a faculty-led committee reviewed his use of AI in published writing. The dispute began in August, when Semafor reported that the AI detector Pangram flagged a *Washington Post* op-ed he had written. The student newspaper, *The Dartmouth*, later ran the same detector on nine of his 2026 articles. [3]

In September, President Sian Leah Beilock appointed a committee of faculty and alumni to review his AI use. It concluded that Schnell “used AI to meaningfully alter his writing in violation of at least one publication’s AI use and/or disclosure requirement, failed to disclose his use of AI in clear violation of a national newspaper’s stated policy, was not forthcoming about his AI use,” and fell short of Dartmouth’s ethics, honor and professionalism standards. [1, 2] He resigned less than two weeks before a scheduled faculty vote of no confidence. Matthew Delmont will serve as interim provost through June 2028. [1, 3]

The committee did not rely on detectors. According to Inside Higher Ed, it compared his articles with the publishers’ AI policies and reviewed logs from his Dartmouth enterprise Claude account. In one exchange he asked Claude whether a manuscript showed “many signatures of AI writing,” then submitted a revised manuscript that incorporated the kinds of changes Claude suggested. He provided his university laptop but did not provide logs from personal AI accounts. [3]

The standard applied was disclosure and candor, which is also what many syllabi require of students. Beilock wrote that leaders must “model the transparency and human judgment that we expect from our students and scholars.” [1] The case also shows that university-licensed AI accounts keep records that can be reviewed later.

## Geneseo: A Vendor Writes to Students

On October 19, the chief executive of an AI tutoring company emailed more than 200 students at SUNY Geneseo. The Chronicle’s headline summarizes the message: “Don’t Worry About Professors’ Rules.” Faculty raised concerns about academic freedom. Provost Mary Toale said she had neither approved nor known about the email, and on October 20 she affirmed to faculty that a course’s instructor decides whether, and how, students may use AI. [4] The full article is paywalled; we have not confirmed the company’s identity, its relationship with the college or the email’s wording.

## Assessment Moves Into the Classroom

**Princeton.** In his August teaching memo, Dean of the College Michael Gordin asked faculty to reverse the usual default: “unless you have an affirmative pedagogical reason to introduce networked digital devices into the classroom, don’t.” The First-Year Seminar program is piloting analog classrooms, with paper and pencil in place of laptops and phones, in courses that include FRS 107: The Rhetoric of Business. Proctoring has returned for in-person exams, and some courses use oral exams. Instructors in one environmental course describe a shift “from evaluating outcomes to evaluating the process.” Student surveys at the end of the semester will decide whether the pilot continues. [5]

**Stanford.** The required COLLEGE program is bringing back in-class written responses and weekly reading quizzes meant to “verify that students are reading the original texts” rather than AI summaries. The vice provost for undergraduate education says students proposed the low-stakes quizzes. [6] Separately, Stanford’s discipline office asked the Faculty Senate to study a retake policy that removes the record of an academic violation, because it may signal that AI violations are minor. No change was proposed. [7]

**Berkeley.** Richard Borcherds now gives homework credit for completion rather than correctness. He told Newsweek that AI “can now solve many undergraduate mathematics problems, often better than the students themselves,” and that “in-person exams still work fine for assessing students.” MIT’s Eric Klopfer added that homework “is no longer a valid evaluation” of learning for some students, and that reducing its weight can also reduce students’ effort. [8, 9]

These are reports of current practice. None of them measures effects on learning.

## What This Means for Isenberg

Our Capstone tests and the campus reports point the same way. Work that certifies a student’s skill should be done where an instructor can see it, and unsupervised work is better treated as practice or graded for process. Colleagues who use simulations, case write-ups or take-home analyses may want to state which work is practice and which is assessed, and to proctor the assessed portion. Dartmouth adds a reminder for the whole department: disclosure rules apply to faculty and administrators as well as students, and university AI accounts keep records. We would welcome colleagues who want to compare notes on simulation assessment.
