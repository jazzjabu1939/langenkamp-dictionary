## Executive Summary

AI tools can help developers study existing programs and build alternatives. Last week’s updates to **Reverse Engineer Anything (REA)** extend that ability. Separate projects are rebuilding familiar Adobe tools. Business schools may also be able to build and adapt their own teaching software. The cost of writing it, however, is only part of the cost of running it.

## REA: Studying Existing Software

**[REA](https://github.com/morluto/rea)** gives AI agents access to software-analysis tools such as Ghidra, Hopper and IDA. An agent can inspect a program’s functions, trace their connections and use the findings to write and test a new implementation. MCP, the interface connecting agent and tools, makes this work possible within a coding session. [3, 4]

A developer can therefore ask how a feature works, rather than ask the agent to guess from its appearance. REA’s **6.5 release, published October 14**, expanded JavaScript analysis and added ways to inspect database snapshots and other program files. The project predates last week; the update extends its reach. [4]

## Rebuilding Creative Tools

The ArtCraft team’s separate [Crafting Apps](https://getartcraft.com/apps) include:

- **[PhotoCraft](https://github.com/storytold/photocraft):** a self-described clean-room Photoshop reimplementation, with layers, masks, brushes and PSD-file support.
- **[VectorCraft](https://github.com/storytold/vectorcraft):** vector illustration targeting Illustrator-style work.
- **[FilmCraft](https://github.com/storytold/filmcraft):** video editing targeting Premiere-style work.

Other projects cover photo development, motion graphics and page layout. Installers are available, though the developers label the programs early alpha or in development. File compatibility and dependable professional use still need testing. [5, 6]

These projects show two approaches to rebuilding software: REA helps an agent investigate a program; the ArtCraft team describes its work as clean-room reimplementation. We found no evidence that REA was used to build the Crafting Apps. [5, 6]

## A School-Run Business Simulation

A school could use similar methods to build its own business simulation. We have already reproduced some **[Capstone-style functions in a working prototype](https://langenkamp.io/strategy-lab/)**, though substantial development and validation remain. [8] REA could help inspect available program files; it cannot recover a simulation engine held only on a vendor’s server.

Building the simulation would leave the school with continuing responsibilities:

- **Maintenance:** host the system, fix faults, update software, support users and test backups and recovery.
- **Records:** preserve student decisions, results and assessment evidence across cohorts, with controlled access, reliable exports and a retention schedule consistent with university policy.
- **Accreditation evidence:** connect results to learning objectives and document how findings improve the curriculum. AACSB’s assurance-of-learning standard calls for documented assessment and improvement; a collection of game scores alone would not meet that purpose. [7]

Student fees might fall, or part of the fee could support hosting, maintenance and record keeping. Savings would depend on the full operating cost, including staff time. A school-run system would need a budget and an owner, not just a developer.

## Implications for Isenberg

Control over the simulation could be as useful as a lower price. Isenberg could add scenarios, decisions and assessments suited to its courses, connect the system to its teaching and record-keeping tools, and revise it as its pedagogy changes. The choice would be whether that flexibility justifies the work of maintaining a dependable service over time.
