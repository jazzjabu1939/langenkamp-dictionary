# Simulacrum

Copenhagen, November 2026. My boots were killing me, so I bought a pair of shoes on Istedgade for 1,400 kroner: black pebbled leather, a stitched moc toe, a chunky sole. They are comfortable, and I have nothing like them at home. They are also, as Thea pointed out within about a minute, a respectful copy of the Paraboot Michael, a French work shoe from the post-war years. I said they were simulacra, as Gibson would say. Thea said the word was really Baudrillard's. We were both right, which is the best outcome for a dictionary.

## In one sentence

**A simulacrum is a copy or likeness, especially one that stands in for an original that has weakened or vanished.**

## The word

The Latin *simulacrum* means a likeness or image, from *simulare*, to make like. It has been in English since about 1600, at first for statues and portraits, and fairly soon with a hint of the counterfeit. A simulacrum resembles something; the question it always raises is how much of the original survived the resembling.

## Baudrillard

The French philosopher Jean Baudrillard gave the word its modern edge in *Simulacra and Simulation* (1981).[1] His argument, compressed: images used to point at real things. Over time they come to disguise the real thing, then to disguise the fact that there is no longer a real thing behind them, and finally to stand on their own with no original at all. His favourite example was Disneyland, which he thought existed partly to persuade Americans that the rest of the country was real.

Baudrillard is easy to overstate and easy to dismiss. The useful core is modest: in a world full of copies, people can lose track of whether there was ever an original, and the copies can start to set the standard the originals are judged by. Readers who know *The Matrix* (1999) have seen the book. Neo hides his contraband disks in a hollowed-out copy of it.

## Gibson

William Gibson put the idea into a shopping trip. In *Pattern Recognition* (2003), Cayce Pollard, a consultant with a near-allergic sensitivity to brands, walks through a London department store and meets a wall of Tommy Hilfiger:

> "This stuff is simulacra of simulacra of simulacra. A diluted tincture of Ralph Lauren, who had himself diluted the glory days of Brooks Brothers, who themselves had stepped on the product of Jermyn Street and Savile Row..."[2]

Gibson calls Tommy "the null point, the black hole," the place beyond which nothing can be more derivative. It is the best account I know of what happens to a style as it passes from hand to hand: each copy keeps the look and loses a little of the reason for it.

My shoes have a shorter chain. A good cut, one or two steps from the source, and they do not hurt. Not every copy is Tommy.

## The AI version

The idea has become practical. Large language models are trained on text from the internet, and a growing share of that text is now written by language models. In 2024 a group of researchers led by Ilia Shumailov showed in *Nature* what happens when models are trained, generation after generation, on the output of earlier models: the rare and unusual parts of the original data disappear first, and the outputs drift toward a bland middle that no longer resembles the human material they started from.[3] They called it *model collapse*. Cayce would have called it Tommy.

The same worry applies to writing by people. A student who learns to write by reading AI-generated prose is learning from a copy of a copy. The [Zombie Internet](/entries/zombie-internet/) and [AI Writing](/entries/ai-writing/) entries take up what that does to the medium.

## Why this matters in a teaching context

Students now meet copies far more often than originals: summaries of the book, explainer videos of the summary, AI answers built from the explainers. The habit worth teaching is Cayce's, without the allergy: ask how many steps a thing is from its source, and go back up the chain when it matters. Read the case, not the summary. Watch the interview, not the clip. Check the footnote.

That is also the method of this Dictionary. Each entry tries to go back to the original source, say what it actually said, and mark plainly where our own extension begins.

## Trade-offs and warnings

- **Copying is how culture works.** Every style borrows, as Gibson's own chain from Savile Row to Brooks Brothers shows. A simulacrum is not automatically a fraud, and treating every copy as decline is snobbery with a theory attached.
- **"No original" is a strong claim.** Baudrillard's final stage, a copy with nothing behind it, is a provocation more than a finding. Most things still have sources, even if they are hard to find.
- **The word can become a pose.** Calling something a simulacrum sounds clever. The useful move is to say what was lost in the copying, and whether it mattered.
