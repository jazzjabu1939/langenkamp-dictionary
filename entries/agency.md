# Agency

Every September a new group of students arrives who have been told, for most of their lives, what to read, when to hand it in, and how it will be marked. By November the good ones have started to do something else. They choose a country for the final project because they want to understand it, not because it was on a list. They notice that the case's numbers do not add up and ask why. Nobody can assign that change. It is the thing a course is for.

This Dictionary uses the word *agency* nearly forty times, across nineteen entries, without ever defining it. Time to pay the debt.

## In one sentence

**Agency is the capacity to act on purpose and answer for the result.**

## Three meanings

The word now carries three meanings at once.

**The human capacity.** In psychology and sociology, agency is a person's ability to set goals, act on them, and shape their circumstances rather than merely being shaped by them. Albert Bandura, the Stanford psychologist, built a career on the observation that people who believe they can affect outcomes act differently from people who do not, and that the belief can be learned.[1] Sociologists set agency against *structure*: the rules, institutions, and habits that limit what any individual can do.

**The legal and economic relationship.** In law, an *agency* is the relationship in which one party, the agent, acts on behalf of another, the principal, under the principal's control.[2] Economists study what goes wrong when the agent's interests differ from the principal's. Michael Jensen and William Meckling's 1976 paper on *agency costs* made the manager-shareholder version of that problem a foundation of corporate finance.[3] This sense is covered in the Dictionary's [Agent](/entries/agent/) entry.

**The property of software.** Since about 2024, *agentic* has described AI systems that pursue goals through several steps, use tools, and act without a human approving each move. The industry chose the word deliberately. It borrows the second meaning (acting on someone's behalf) and quietly implies some of the first (acting on purpose).

## Where the meanings meet

The three senses were mostly separate until recently. A person had agency; a firm had agents; software had features.

Agentic software joins them in a way that should interest anyone who teaches. When a student hands a task to an agent, the legal sense is in play: the agent acts on the student's behalf and the student answers for what it does. But the human sense is in play as well. Each task handed over is a task the student did not perform, and some of those tasks were the ones through which the student would have learned to act on purpose.

That is the Dictionary's own extension of the term, and it should be stated plainly as such: **delegation to machines can increase a person's agency or hollow it out, depending on whether the person still sets the purpose and still checks the result.** A student who uses an agent to gather fifty company filings, then decides which three matter and why, has more agency than before. A student who asks an agent for the answer and submits it has handed over the part that was theirs.

## A working test

Three questions tell the two cases apart:

1. **Whose purpose is it?** Did the person choose the goal, or accept the one the tool suggested?
2. **Who checked?** Could the person explain why the result is right, and did they look?
3. **Who answers?** If the result is wrong, does the person treat it as their error, or the tool's?

A yes to all three is agency extended by a tool. A no to all three is agency outsourced. Most real use sits in between, which is why the questions are worth asking each time.

## Why this matters in a teaching context

The usual worry about AI in education is cheating. The deeper concern is agency. A course can catch a copied essay. It is much harder to notice that a student has stopped forming purposes of their own because something always offers one first.

The encouraging part is Bandura's: agency is learned through doing, and it can be taught. Students who use agents well, who set the goal, inspect the output, and own the mistakes, are practising exactly the capacity a business education is supposed to build. The aim is to have them handle these tools often, with their own hands on the purpose.

## Trade-offs and warnings

- **The software sense flatters the software.** Calling a program *agentic* implies purposes it does not have. Its goals are supplied by people, through prompts, training, and product design. The [Anthropomorphising Agent Behaviour](/entries/anthropomorphising-agent-behaviour/) entry covers the temptation.
- **The human sense can be romanticised.** Structure is real. A student working two jobs has less room to act on purpose than one who is not, and no essay on agency changes that.
- **The test is a habit, not a rule.** No institution can audit whose purpose each piece of work served. The questions only help if people ask them of themselves.

William Gibson called his 2020 novel *Agency*, and the title carries all three meanings at once: an AI acting on its own judgment, the people who deploy it, and the characters trying to keep some say over their own lives. The [Agent](/entries/agent/) entry takes up the novel in detail.
