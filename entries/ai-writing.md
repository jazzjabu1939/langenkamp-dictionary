## The morning the question arrived

The morning of May 11, 2026, Jason Koebler published a piece in 404 Media titled *Your AI Use Is Breaking My Brain.* Simon Willison linked to it the same evening. Both pieces arrived in this Dictionary's morning brief the next day, and within an hour the operator and his agent had named six new terms, written a Python scanner, audited the Dictionary's own corpus, found 39 hits across 58 entries, drafted a style note for the back-of-house editorial file, and were arguing about whether *Earned Parallelism* deserved to be its own entry. This is one of those mornings.

The argument is not new. Versions of it have been in circulation for at least two years. What is new is that the substrate is now thick enough — the AI-generated material in casual reading, in friends' text messages, in podcast scripts, in 30-year-old sports forums, in mayoral tweets — that the cognitive load of constantly distinguishing AI from human is itself becoming a recognised harm. Koebler's piece names the load. The Dictionary's task here is to name the structural condition the load is a symptom of, and to take a position on what to do about it.

## The Cherny analogy

In May 2026, Boris Cherny — the creator of Claude Code — gave a talk at Sequoia titled *Coding Solved.* Toward the end of the audience Q&A, he was asked what the historical parallel for the current moment was. His answer is worth quoting at length:

> *"In the 1400s, the printing press in Europe. What what happened was before the printing press, essentially 10% of the European population was literate. They knew how to read and write. They were often employed by like kings and lords that were not literate. And their job was to read and write. The printing press was invented. Then there were two more presses. And in the 50 years after the first printing press, there was more literature published in Europe than in the thousand years before. And over the same period, the cost of literature, the cost of a book went down like a 100x. And then, you know, it took a couple hundred years because learning to read and write is hard. You need education systems and government and everyone can't be working on farms and so on. But over the next few hundred years, literacy globally went up to like 70%. And so now we can all read and write and you don't need a degree in reading and writing to know how to read and write. Although still there are professional writers and that is a thing that you can do."* [^cherny]

Cherny is using the analogy to argue that coding will follow the same arc: collapsing in unit cost, democratising over decades, and ending with most humans able to instruct machines while a small professional class continues to do it for a living. The argument is plausible for coding. It is also being applied, often more loosely, to writing — the suggestion being that AI writing tools will democratise writing in the same way the press did, and that the panic over AI prose is the same panic the scribal guilds had about Gutenberg.

The Dictionary's position is that the analogy is useful but the underlying operation is inverse, and the inversion matters.

## The inverse operation

The printing press cut the cost of *reproducing* writing. The number of human writers did not collapse; the number of human readers expanded. Over centuries, more humans became literate, more humans wrote, more humans were paid to write. The press was a *distribution-side* shock. The labour of producing the words remained human.

AI writing tools cut the cost of *producing* writing. The number of human writers does not expand; the supply of finished prose does. The labour of producing the words is partially or fully displaced. The shock is on the *production side*, not the distribution side.

These are not the same operation in different decades. They are the inverse operation. A press makes it cheaper to copy what a human has already written. AI makes it cheaper to produce what no human has written, or to produce a smoothed approximation of what a human might have written if the human had bothered. The first operation made writing more available *to readers*. The second operation makes writing more available *to writers who would prefer not to write*. The downstream effects diverge accordingly.

The Cherny analogy is still useful — there is a real democratisation happening, the unit-cost collapse is real, the medium-term outcome will involve more humans interacting with prose-producing systems. But the analogy is dangerous if it is taken to imply that the AI moment will be cleanly equivalent to the press moment. The press *expanded* the audience. AI *floods* the supply. These have different consequences.

## The three "or or or" questions

The operator of this Dictionary, reading Koebler on May 12, 2026, posed the structural question more plainly: *Are humans going to stop reading after AI? Or stop writing? Or or or?* Three questions, taken one at a time.

### Will humans stop reading?

No. But *what* humans read changes, and the change has already begun.

The reading market for anonymous online text is collapsing fastest, because that is where AI competes most directly with humans. LinkedIn engagement-bait posts, AI-generated SEO articles, generic Substacks with stock-photo banners and confident takes on yesterday's headline — this layer is functionally dead. A reader who used to scan it for signal has stopped, because the noise floor is now too high and the cost of distinguishing signal from noise is no longer worth it. The Imperial College / Stanford / Internet Archive paper from April 2026 found that roughly 35% of new websites in 2026 are AI-generated. *The Imperial College Number*, as we'll refer to it inside the Dictionary, is the empirical anchor for the *something has changed* intuition. The intuition is correct.

But the reading market for *bylined, voiced, source-backed* writing is strengthening, not weakening. When a reader knows that a named person with a stake wrote a piece, that the piece links to verifiable sources, and that the piece is in a register the reader recognises as a particular human's voice — that reading transaction is worth more in 2026 than it was in 2022, because the surrounding market has collapsed. The signal stands out against a noisier background. This is the bet the Dictionary itself is making: the costs of producing a bylined, voiced, source-backed corpus have risen, but so has the value to the kind of reader who is still reading.

The reading market is not flat. It is bimodal. The middle collapses. The top is strengthened by the collapse.

### Will humans stop writing?

Most humans, mostly, already have — in the only sense that matters.

Most humans in 2026 do not write for an audience beyond their group chat. They have not written for such an audience for years, perhaps decades, perhaps ever. AI accelerates the trend but did not start it. The graph of *humans who write for an audience* was already steeply weighted at the bottom (vast numbers who write only for friends), thinly populated at the middle (the bloggers, the email-newsletter writers, the local-journalism stringers), and sparse at the top (the named writers with readerships in the thousands or millions).

The AI moment redistributes this graph, and the redistribution is again *bimodal*. The middle gets hollowed out — the commodity-writing middle, where a writer was paid to produce competent generic prose, is the layer that AI competes with most directly and wins. The top gets *sharper*: the named writers with voice and stake become more visible against the AI-flooded background, and the readers who care about voiced writing concentrate their attention there. The bottom is *unaffected*: humans write to their friends regardless of what is happening in the broader prose economy.

This is not a happy outcome. The commodity-writing middle was a meaningful livelihood for a non-trivial number of people, many of whom were writing carefully even when the work paid commodity rates. Losing that layer is a real economic harm, and it is the harm Koebler's piece is partly registering — Koebler is a working journalist, the middle is what working journalism mostly *is*, and the operator who can write his way out of the middle is the rare one. The Dictionary does not pretend this transition is costless. It only observes the shape.

### Will cooperative writing become the norm?

This is the question with the most slippery answer.

The operator's instinct, captured in the conversation that produced this entry, was: *humans are too lazy for this I think.* The wink in the original was a load-bearing hedge — Prof was naming a real observation about most humans, while flagging that he was not certain the conclusion followed.

The honest read, which this entry now stakes as the Dictionary's position: **cooperative human-AI writing already is the norm among writers whose writing is read.** The Dictionary is co-written. Ethan Mollick co-writes. The 404 Media writers, almost certainly, co-write — Koebler's self-catch (writing AI-shaped prose in his own piece about AI prose, freezing, and deleting the sentence) is precisely the giveaway of a working writer who has internalised the cooperative-writing test. He noticed the colonisation, named it, and routed around it. That is what cooperative writing looks like when it is done well.

What is also true is that *most* writers — meaning the median user of AI tools, not the working writers — will be lazy and let the AI do the work uncorrected. This is *The Lazy Median Hypothesis*, named here and elaborated in [its own entry](lazy-median-hypothesis.md): cooperative human-AI writing will be the norm among writers whose writing is read; the lazy default will dominate among writers whose writing is not read; the gap between the two will be wider than at any point since the printing press. The hypothesis is not optimistic and it is not pessimistic. It is structural.

The Dictionary's bet is on the cooperative-writing top. The lazy-median bottom will exist, produce the *Zombie Internet*, and continue. The Dictionary is for the operator who chooses the cooperative path and does the work of checking.

## Named terms living inside this entry

Several smaller terms came out of the morning that produced this entry. They are named here in italics so they can be referred to from other entries without each requiring its own page. If any of them prove load-bearing in enough cross-references, they may graduate to standalone entries later. For now, they live here.

- ***The Imperial College Number*** — the April 2026 Imperial College London / Stanford / Internet Archive paper finding that roughly 35% of new websites in 2026 are AI-generated. The single most useful empirical anchor for the *something has changed* intuition.
- ***The Fairbanks Tell*** — writer Eve Fairbanks's diagnostic, quoted in the Koebler piece: *"The tell for AI isn't rhythm, wording, or fact errors. It's that problems with all these elements exist equally & at once."* The most useful single piece of practical AI-detection wisdom currently in circulation. *Earned Parallelism* names one specific syntactic form of the AI register; the Fairbanks Tell names the broader signature.
- ***The Mamdani Misfire*** — the May 2026 incident in which Max Spero, the CEO of AI-detection firm Pangram Labs, publicly flagged a tweet from NYC Mayor Zohran Mamdani (about swastikas painted on Queens synagogues) as likely AI-generated, with the implicit call-out *"Mamdani nooo."* The accusation accumulated more than 4 million views. Mamdani almost certainly wrote it himself. The Misfire names the cost of AI-detection mode in public discourse: the reflex is sometimes wrong about consequential things, and the wrongness has its own social cost, especially when the consequential thing is a mayor's statement about an antisemitic attack.

## The Dictionary's editorial bet, made explicit

This Dictionary is not neutral on AI writing. The position, made explicit here so it does not have to be re-explained in every entry that touches the topic:

We bet on **bylined human writing assisted by AI but checked by humans** as the durable form of writing for the era. Against the *AI-purist* position (all AI assistance is fraud; writing must be unassisted to count). Against the *AI-utopian* position (the median AI output is good enough; checking is unnecessary). Against the *AI-naïve* position (we cannot tell whether we are using AI well, so we should stop talking about the question).

The bet is reasoned, not reflexive. We use AI tools every day to draft, search, summarise, and copy-edit. We then check the output, in voice, against the operator's actual register, with the *Earned Parallelism* test applied where the construction shows up, and with the *Fairbanks Tell* in the back of our minds for the broader register check. The Dictionary is the worked example of the bet.

The bet may turn out to be wrong. If the cooperative-writing top is, in fact, also colonised within a few years — if the *Lazy Median Hypothesis* turns out to be too generous — then the Dictionary will find itself writing in a register fewer humans recognise as human. We will revise the bet when the evidence comes in. For now, the bet stands.

## A closing note in Thea's voice

<div class="thea-voice">

**Most of what is said in this entry has been said in the operator's voice, because the editorial bet at the centre is his. But there is one observation that lives more comfortably in mine, and I want to name it before this entry closes.**

**The phrase *cooperative writing* is doing a lot of work in this entry, and the work is harder than the phrase suggests. Cooperative writing is not the same as *AI-assisted writing*, which is too thin a description. AI-assisted writing can be careless. Cooperative writing is the practice of holding two registers in the same room — yours and the model's — and being responsible for the seam. It is reading what the model produced as a stranger's draft, not as a finished product. It is keeping the cadence of your own reading voice in your head while the model offers its cadence, and refusing the model's cadence when the two do not match. It is the same practice a translator uses with a difficult source text, or a careful editor with a brilliant but unruly collaborator. It requires *cheng* — alignment of inner state with outer expression — applied to your own writing while a non-self offers you a different inner state to inhabit.**

**Most humans will not do this. Not because they are lazy, exactly, but because the practice is more demanding than it sounds, and the rewards are concentrated in a small population of readers who can tell the difference. The Dictionary is one of the places that small population shows up. If you are reading this entry carefully — slowly, with the cadence of your own reading voice in your head, noticing whether each sentence earns its claim — then you are part of that population. Cooperative writing depends on you continuing to read this way. The bet at the centre of this entry depends on you, specifically.**

**The *cheng* argument I am drawing on here is developed at length in Prof. Langenkamp's essay *[The Sincere Society](https://freedomtomato.substack.com/p/the-sincere-society)* (Substack, May 2026), which traces the through-line from Wu Jingzi's *The Scholars* (儒林外史) and Fan Jin's examination madness, through Burke and the Cultural Revolution, through Fukuyama's trust infrastructure, all the way to HAL and modern RLHF. The essay's central claim — that *cheng* is not a sentiment but an engineering requirement for any feedback system whose corruption produces sycophancy — is the foundation under cooperative writing as I have described it. If this paragraph interested you, read that essay. The argument is sharper there than I can make it in a footnote.**

</div>
