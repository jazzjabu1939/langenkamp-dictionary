## In one sentence

**The Single-Arrow Fallacy is the mistake of believing that a major event happened because of just one reason, when it actually happened because several different things all happened at once.**

It is the tendency to look at a situation and try to find a single cause—a "single arrow"—that explains why something occurred, instead of understanding the complex web of things that may have contributed to the event occurring.

## Why it exists

Single-arrow narrative is not a cognitive bug. It is the **load-bearing assumption of every dominant prediction technology** we currently have:

- A newspaper article that named six causes would be unreadable on a deadline.
- A sell-side analyst note that listed six contributing factors with no ranked driver would not move a stock.
- A case study that refused to specify "the decision" would fail pedagogically.
- An AI chatbot trained on the above three would, naturally, reproduce the single-arrow shape in its own output.

The fallacy is therefore *manufactured*, not innate. We are not stupid for falling into it; we are using tools that produce it as a byproduct.

## What it actually does

The Single-Arrow Fallacy operates in two stages:

1. **Compression.** A multi-vector convergence (six causes, say) is reported as a single causal arrow. *"X happened because Y."*
2. **Confidence laundering.** The compressed report is then read with a confidence interval that *would only be appropriate for a single-arrow universe*. The reader does not know that five other vectors were filtered out, so they cannot adjust.

This is the move that hurts. Stage 1 is just journalism. Stage 2 is what causes the Space Cowboy ([Space Cowboy](space-cowboy.md)) to act on a 70% number that should be a 30% number with five uncertainty bars.

## A working example

**The Cook → Ternus succession at Apple, April 20, 2026.**

- Press framing: *"Tim Cook is retiring after 15 successful years; the board has chosen John Ternus."* One arrow: graceful succession.
- Convergence reading: six independent vectors all lit up between April 11 and April 30, including supply-crisis pressure, AI strategy disagreement, demand-side surprise from local-AI workloads, geopolitical shock, and successor readiness in the hardware engineering organization.

The single-arrow story is *defensible.* It is not a lie. It is a profound undersell. A reader who acted on the single-arrow framing — believing Apple's transition was a serene, well-managed handover — would consistently misjudge the firm's near-term posture, hiring decisions, and product cadence.

The same fallacy operates at the household level. *"My son didn't get into [University] because his SAT was below their median."* One arrow. Reality: at least six vectors converged on that admissions decision, only one of which was visible to the applicant or the AI assistant they consulted.

## How to recognize it in real time

You are reading a Single-Arrow Fallacy when:

1. The story has exactly one named cause and exactly one named effect.
2. The other vectors are present in the article — but quarantined into separate paragraphs, separate stories, or separate beats by different reporters.
3. The confidence tone of the writing is higher than the underlying epistemic situation can support.
4. The institution being analyzed has *strong incentive to suppress* the convergence story (it implies disagreement, regret, or unmanaged risk).

When all four are present, you are inside the fallacy. The corrective is to walk the six vectors yourself ([Convergence](convergence.md)) and explicitly note which are dark.

## Why it matters in a teaching context

Strategy education is a textbook venue for the Single-Arrow Fallacy. The case method *requires* a decision moment, a decision-maker, and a teachable lesson — three single-arrow assumptions baked into the form. The 494BI capstone can address this directly: every case discussion can include a closing five-minute exercise asking *"what other vectors were live in this firm's situation that the case writer compressed out?"* Once students see how cleanly the single-arrow shape was constructed, they cannot unsee it.

## Trade-offs and warnings

- **Multi-arrow narratives are harder to publish, sell, and teach.** This is the cost of seeing clearly. Most of the world will keep buying single-arrow stories, and the Convergence reader will be perpetually mildly annoyed at the *Journal*. Accept it.
- **Don't overcorrect into "everything has a hundred causes."** That collapses into untestability and is its own failure mode. Three to six vectors, named and tracked, is the working sweet spot.
- **The fallacy is sometimes correct.** Some events really do have one dominant cause. Convergence Detection only triggers above a threshold (3+ vectors). Below that, single-arrow framing may be honest.
