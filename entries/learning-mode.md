A **learning mode** is a setting that makes an AI assistant act as a tutor. With the setting on, the assistant asks questions, gives hints, and checks what the student understands; with it off, the same assistant hands over a finished answer. Capitalised, Learning mode is Anthropic's name for the feature in Claude. In lower case the phrase covers the same kind of feature in other products.

Anthropic introduced Learning mode on April 2, 2025, as part of [Claude for Education](https://www.anthropic.com/news/introducing-claude-for-education). The announcement says Claude “guides students' reasoning process rather than providing answers,” asks questions such as “How would you approach this problem?” and “What evidence supports your conclusion?”, and draws attention to the principle behind a specific problem. In August 2025 the company opened the feature to all Claude users and added a version for programmers in Claude Code. OpenAI's Study Mode and Google's Guided Learning arrived the same summer.

The Anthropic education team told the origin story in a December 2025 conversation, [*What does AI mean for education?*](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Uh98_aGhAuY). Educators kept asking, “Where's your learning mode?” Students supplied the reason, in their own word: *[Brain Rot](/entries/brain-rot/)*. They could already get an assignment finished. They wanted help that left them able to sit the midterm. The team says the first version was built in about two weeks. It can also turn the course material a student uploads into flashcards for exam preparation.

A learning mode changes what the tool does by default. The answer machine is still there: the student can switch the mode off or open another tab. So the feature helps the student who has already decided to learn, and it cannot make that decision for anyone. The instructor's part is unchanged: an assignment worth doing properly, and some *[Proof of Learning](/entries/proof-of-learning/)* beyond the submitted artifact.

Early evidence favours the design. In a field experiment with high-school mathematics students, those given an ordinary GPT-4 chat interface scored 17 per cent worse than the control group on a later exam taken without it, while those given a tutor version that offered hints and withheld answers showed no such loss ([Bastani et al., *PNAS*, 2025](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2422633122)). The study tested the researchers' own tutor, and no commercial product; the commercial learning modes still need trials of their own.
