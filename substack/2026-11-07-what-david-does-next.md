# What David Does Next
*A new majority, a cracking market and the lessons of the 1920s: a case for a centrism that may represent intellectual humility.*

Democrats won big on Tuesday. They won the House by a wide margin and took back the Senate, and they won governorships in places that a year ago would have earned you a pitying look at the faculty club.

It is tempting to read the result as a vindication of the party. I think that would be a costly mistake.

Look at who won. Many of them are very capable people, and the best of them understand that their first duty is to their constituents. That, more than the party label, is the strength of this group. Glenn Hubbard, who chaired the Council of Economic Advisers under George W. Bush, said as much on Bloomberg's *Wall Street Week* this week: "There's a view that well the Democrats won because their ideas are better. Well, they may or may not be, but that's not what the electorate was saying." Voters, he said, want "what works." It is useful to hear that from a Republican economist, and I agree with him.

My favorite of the winners is Elaine Luria, who won back Virginia's Second District, the seat she lost in 2022. She is perhaps the least charismatic person in the new Congress, which is exactly why I like her. On election night she said the goal was "to bring us back to the center, to civility and to working together." Asked by NBC whether impeaching the President was a priority, she said it was "not our priority. Our priority is to deliver for the American people." Then she said the thing I wish more people in both parties would tattoo somewhere visible: "If the person in the White House is of a different party, you got to find ways to work with them. If they're of the same party, I don't have any problem standing up to them."

In Texas, James Talarico won a Senate seat for the Democrats for the first time in nearly forty years. He looks like Richie Cunningham from *Happy Days* and used to teach language arts at a middle school on the west side of San Antonio, which he calls "the best preparation for politics." His coalition included first-time voters and, in his words, "three-time Trump voters." His first promise on election night was to the other side: "To the Texans who didn't vote for me, I will be your senator, too."

These people did not appear by accident. A large, messy and much-doubted system produced them. The election ran cleanly. Robert Post, former dean of the Yale Law School, told Bloomberg he was astonished at how relieved he felt that "people just voted and their votes were reported honestly and fairly." County clerks and state officials did their jobs under pressure. Elon Musk spent nearly half a billion dollars trying to keep Republicans in power and bought very little with it. Democracies everywhere should take heart from that. Let the people vote, and they will often choose well.

## What they actually ran on

The victory speeches and interviews dwell less on ideology than on practical matters: what things cost and whether government works.

The cost of living came first. Bill Clinton, campaigning in Florida for David Jolly, borrowed a line he had heard from another speaker and promised to credit every time he used it: "Not about woke, it's about broke." He was "tired of politicians picking fights when the voters are trying to pick what they got to put back on the shelf at the grocery store."

But the list went well beyond groceries. It included the preparedness of our military and the management of the Pentagon, where Luria, a retired Navy commander whose district holds the largest naval base in the world, raised questions about readiness in the Gulf that she said the current Congress had not asked. It included the basic operation of government, the violation of people's rights, and cuts to benefits that people had planned their lives around, such as Medicaid and the ACA premium subsidies.

It also included the rule of law and corruption. These were not side issues. Voters noticed a government that, in Post's words, "acts first and then attempts to explain to a court later," and a Justice Department that he believes has been politicized. "If I were a business person," he said, "I would be terrified." He is right to put it in business terms. Every contract depends on courts that apply the law the same way to everyone, and a Treasury bond is, as he put it, a promise to repay. Corruption is a tax on every honest transaction.

These are problems of a highly complex economy and a highly complex government. Hubbard's diagnosis is blunt: "Politicians on both sides of the aisle have nodded, but have not offered solutions. And I think voters are sick of it." Slogans from the left or the right will not solve such problems; they need people who have done the reading.

I should be honest about one result: Jolly lost. Byron Donalds beat him by a little over a point in a state Republicans have run for three decades. Clinton's message came close in hard country. It did not win everywhere, and we should not pretend otherwise.

## 1928 and 1930

The year I keep returning to is 1930, not 1932.

In the 1930 midterms, a year after the Crash, Democrats gained about fifty House seats, and even then Republicans kept a one-seat edge on election night. Democrats took the House only after a run of special elections, and John Nance Garner did not become Speaker until December 1931. Republicans narrowly held the Senate. The new majority had a mandate. It did not yet have a program. What it did with the next two years shaped the following generation of American politics.

The parallel has limits, and I should state them. The Democrats of 1930 were elected a year after the Crash. Our market looks more like that of 1928. That year Herbert Hoover won the presidency in a landslide on Coolidge prosperity, and stocks seemed to go only up. Through most of 2026 the stock market has been very good, carried by the AI boom, and this week it has shown signs of cracking. We do not know whether a crash like 1929's is coming, and we should hope it is not. In politics we may be in 1930; in the markets we may be closer to 1928.

The second limit concerns cause. The familiar story says the Crash caused the Great Depression. Many economists no longer accept that. Since Milton Friedman and Anna Schwartz in the 1960s, and later Ben Bernanke and Barry Eichengreen, they have pointed to other causes: waves of bank failures, a Federal Reserve that let the money supply shrink, the gold standard, and the debts left by the First World War. I return to this below, because it changes what a sensible majority should watch.

In the respect that matters most, we are in the same position as the majority of 1930. Voters have given a mandate; the program still has to be built. There are two years before 2028 to build it, and it will be a better program if the people building it, and the citizens advising them, have read some history first.

## History, sorted honestly

I grew up with the New Dealer version of the 1920s and 1930s, the one our parents gave us. In that story, the Republicans of the twenties were greedy, Hoover sat on his hands while the country starved, and Franklin Roosevelt rode in and saved capitalism from itself.

The more I read about that period, the less I believe the cartoon. The glorification of FDR is somewhat unjustified, and the demonization of the leaders of the late 1920s and early 1930s is overblown. We have a century of good historiography now. Democrats should hear the other side.

**Mellon.** Under Woodrow Wilson, a Democrat, the top income tax rate reached 77 percent in 1918 to pay for the First World War. Andrew Mellon, Treasury Secretary under Harding, Coolidge and Hoover, brought it down to 25 percent by 1926, took millions of small earners off the tax rolls entirely, ran a surplus every year he was in office, and cut the national debt by about a third. His argument, set out in *Taxation: The People's Business* (1924), was that rates that high were not actually being paid. The wealthy parked their money in tax-exempt municipal bonds, so the statutory rate promised revenue that it did not deliver while starving productive investment.

Mellon's reputation now rests heavily on one quotation: "liquidate labor, liquidate stocks, liquidate the farmers, liquidate real estate." We have it from Herbert Hoover's memoirs, written years later by a man with every reason to make his Treasury Secretary the villain of the piece.

**Hoover.** Hoover was not a do-nothing President. He was an activist, and some of his actions did real harm. He called industry leaders to the White House in 1929 and pressed them to hold wages up as prices fell. He signed the Smoot-Hawley tariff in 1930. And with the Revenue Act of 1932 he raised the top tax rate from 25 percent to 63 percent, in the middle of a depression.

**Roosevelt and Mellon.** Then there is what Roosevelt did to Mellon, a story Greg Steinmetz tells in *The President and the Oligarch*, which I came across on the History Unplugged podcast. With unemployment still terrible and Huey Long, Father Coughlin and Francis Townsend pushing him from the left, Roosevelt needed someone to blame, and he chose the third-richest man in America. His Justice Department sought a tax-fraud indictment over Mellon's 1931 return. In May 1934, a Pittsburgh grand jury, the kind of working people who had voted for Roosevelt, refused to indict. The administration took the case to the Board of Tax Appeals anyway, although, by Steinmetz's account, Roosevelt's own advisers told him it was weak. Meanwhile Mellon, old and dying, sat through the hearings and gave the nation the National Gallery of Art. He paid for the paintings, the building and its upkeep, and left his name off the door so other collectors would follow. Mellon died in August 1937. That December, the Board cleared him of fraud, though it held that he owed some of the tax the government claimed.

By Steinmetz's own account, Mellon had done things worth investigating: using the State Department to help Gulf Oil win a concession in Kuwait, continuing to run his businesses while serving as Treasury Secretary, and some evidence of insider trading. Roosevelt pursued a weak charge and left the real conduct alone. In Steinmetz's words, the tax code became "a weapon," and later presidents have used it too.

**Roosevelt and the rules.** The Mellon case was not an isolated lapse. In 1937, fresh from a landslide, Roosevelt proposed adding up to six justices to a Supreme Court that kept striking down his programs. His own party stopped him. The Senate Judiciary Committee, with a Democratic majority, called the plan "a needless, futile and utterly dangerous abandonment of constitutional principle," and the Senate sent it back to committee, 70 to 20. The man who led the fight against it was Burton K. Wheeler, a Democrat from Montana. Earlier, in 1933, Congress at Roosevelt's urging had voided the gold clauses in contracts, including those in the government's own bonds, so that creditors would be repaid in cheaper dollars. The Supreme Court later said that breaking the government's own promise was unconstitutional, and then allowed it to stand. Post's point about Treasuries being a promise to repay is not new. It has been tested before, by a Democrat.

**What the Crash did and did not do.** Steinmetz makes the point I raised earlier, and it is worth taking to heart this week: stock market crashes do not cause depressions. When the dot-com bubble burst, the Nasdaq lost nearly four-fifths of its value and we got a mild recession. Following Friedman and Bernanke, he argues that the Great Depression came from the debts and reparations left by the First World War, from waves of bank failures, and above all from a Federal Reserve that let the money supply collapse.

**Intellectual humility cuts both ways.** Followed to its end, that argument does not land where the revisionists would like. If a shortage of money caused the Depression, then the most important thing anyone did to end it was Roosevelt's decision in 1933 to take the country off the gold standard and let prices recover. That is the argument of Barry Eichengreen's *Golden Fetters* and Christina Romer's "What Ended the Great Depression?", and it is close to the consensus of economic historians. The same devaluation that broke the gold clauses helped end the Depression; history rarely sorts itself into heroes and villains. Mellon, a member of the Federal Reserve Board by virtue of his office, stood on the wrong side of that argument. Steinmetz says the New Deal "did nothing" for unemployment. That goes too far: unemployment fell from about a quarter of the workforce in 1933 to about one in seven by 1937. Deposit insurance and the Securities and Exchange Commission have served the country for ninety years.

But some of the New Deal was not constructive. The National Industrial Recovery Act organized industries into government-blessed cartels that raised prices in a depression. The administration's tax increases on business and income in the mid-1930s, and the premature tightening of money and budgets in 1936 and 1937, helped bring on the sharp relapse of 1937.

So the lesson is not that FDR was wrong. It is to credit what actually worked and drop the hagiography.

**Truman.** Then there is Harry Truman, whom I have been listening to by way of David McCullough's biography. Truman shows how a New Deal heir governed without Roosevelt's prestige. Voters gave him a Republican Congress in 1946. He ran against it in 1948 as the "do-nothing Congress," and won. Yet in between he worked with Arthur Vandenberg, the Republican chairman of the Senate Foreign Relations Committee, to pass the Marshall Plan. His example: argue hard where the parties differ, and build the large things with whoever will vote for them.

## Taxes, wars and arithmetic

Taxation is a large issue, and it is where we Democrats are most likely to forget our history.

We want to preserve incentives and market signals. We do not want to advocate confiscatory rates and a reduction of freedom. The history of the 77 percent top rate is not encouraging.

But we should be precise about Mellon's lesson. His argument was strong at rates of 73 to 77 percent. Today's top federal rate is 37 percent, and the best evidence from his own era is more modest than his admirers claim. Christina and David Romer studied the interwar years precisely because rates swung so wildly. They found that people reported more taxable income when rates fell, but only modestly, and they found no clear effect on investment in machinery or construction, though some suggestive evidence of more new businesses. ([AEJ: Economic Policy, 2014](https://eml.berkeley.edu/~dromer/papers/Romer%20and%20Romer%20Incentive%20Effects%20AEJ%20Policy.pdf)) At today's rates, tax cuts do not pay for themselves. Mellon's lasting lesson is that rates should never be confiscatory, shelters should be closed, and the base should be broad enough that the government collects what it says it will.

Tariffs belong in this arithmetic. Hubbard is plain about them: "Tariffs did raise prices and Americans did bear the burdens of tariffs. Everything your econ 101 professor taught you is still true." Smoot-Hawley taught the same lesson ninety-six years ago. Hubbard also grants that voters worry, with reason, about America's competitiveness, and that cutting tariffs will only sell if it comes with a real answer to the question "How are you going to help me?"

Wars are part of this arithmetic too. Wilson paid for the First World War with punishing rates and heavy borrowing, and Mellon spent a decade paying the bill. We are now paying for a war with Iran, with oil prices that touched $100 a barrel and a national debt of about $40 trillion. Clinton, asked for years how he balanced the budget, has a one-word answer: "Arithmetic."

The benefits people relied on are part of it as well. Hubbard notes that the Social Security trust funds run out in 2032. "Now, those are actually meaningless accounting fictions," he says, "but they matter a lot in Washington." He is right on both counts. The trust funds hold Treasury bonds, so the government owes the money to itself; the real question is whether the economy and the tax base can pay the benefits. But the law ties benefits to the funds, and when they run out, benefits fall automatically to what payroll taxes cover. The accounting is a fiction. The deadline is real, and it falls on the watch of the people just elected. Hubbard adds that interest rates are likely to stay "higher for longer," which sets up what he calls "a negative fiscal loop" as the government refinances its debt.

Chrystia Freeland, Canada's former finance minister, made the same point about Europe on the same program. Borrowing a phrase from Manitoba's premier, Wab Kinew, she said that "the economic horse needs to pull the social cart," and that "if you are a person... who believes in a strong social safety net, you need to also believe in fiscal responsibility." Canada repaired its finances only after what she called a "near-death fiscal experience" in the 1990s. Sensible people in both parties should do the repair before the crisis. Greg Steinmetz ends his interview with the same warning: the bond market will eventually discipline what democracy will not, and the correction will come with inflation and unemployment.

## The AI boom: check it, don't crash it

The comparison with 1929 matters most here.

On the Thursday after the election, the Dow fell more than 1,800 points and the Nasdaq 5.5 percent, its worst day in about eighteen months, "amid concerns," as NBC put it, that "Democrats' big election victories could hinder the AI boom." In Seoul the next morning, the KOSPI fell more than 8 percent at the open and trading was halted for twenty minutes. Voters had been asked directly about data centers. Ballotpedia counted 41 local measures in ten states, most of them designed to limit development, and NBC's exit poll found 76 percent of voters opposed to a data center in their own community.

Before we accept the headline, consider the timing. Dennis Dick, a strategist at the Stock Trader Network in Toronto, told DW News that the AI hardware bubble "actually started bursting probably early October." SanDisk's run this year, roughly tenfold, is steeper than RCA's in 1928. RCA peaked in September 1929 and then lost 98 percent of its value by 1932. SanDisk has already fallen by more than half from its October peak. The new majority arrived in the middle of a deflation that began before anyone voted. Blaming it for the deflation would repeat the habit I described above, in which the newcomer is blamed for the bust and the boom is forgotten.

Meanwhile the rest of the economy looks steadier than the chip stocks. Tom Barkin, president of the Richmond Fed, says demand "still seems to be solid," and that the businesses he visits have strong earnings and margins that are holding up. What they dislike is the uncertainty around them.

On CNN, one panelist described the AI economy as money "going around and around in a handful of companies." Microsoft buys from Nvidia, Nvidia invests in its customers, and they all prepare for their IPOs. Anyone who has read about the investment trusts of the late 1920s, buying shares in one another on borrowed money, will feel a small chill.

Steinmetz's point applies here. If the AI boom deflates, falling share prices will not be the main danger; a market correction need not become a depression. The danger is a policy mistake on top of it: money tightened at the wrong moment, tariffs escalated, or stress in the private credit markets that finance data centers allowed to spread. On Friday a strong jobs report raised the odds that the Federal Reserve will raise rates in December. The Fed tightened in 1928 and 1929 to cool speculation, and Friedman and Schwartz count that among the mistakes that turned a crash into a depression. The lesson of 1929 to 1933 is to watch the banks, credit markets and money supply more closely than the stock index.

So the new majority faces a choice the 1930 majority would recognize. Its voters elected it partly to rein in a speculative boom that is crowding into their towns, raising their power bills and drinking their water. Doing nothing would betray those voters. A clumsy response, such as national moratoriums or a sudden regulatory shock in a fragile market, could make Democrats the party blamed for the bust.

Hoover's lesson for Democrats may be that well-meant activism in a fragile market can do as much damage as inaction. The sensible course is to restrain the boom without breaking it: make the builders pay their own way for power and water, give communities a real voice, and let prices show what this technology is worth.

## Oversight in good faith, not retribution

The incoming chairman of the House Oversight Committee, Robert Garcia, issued a two-word statement on election night: "Lawyer up." I understand the feeling. Oversight is overdue and legitimate.

The 1920s show how to do it well. In 1923 and 1924, Senator Thomas Walsh, a Democrat from Montana, led patient hearings into the leasing of federal oil reserves at Teapot Dome. He followed the evidence to Harding's Interior Secretary, Albert Fall, who was convicted in October 1929 of taking a $100,000 bribe, the first cabinet member convicted of a crime committed in office. Burton Wheeler, the other senator from Montana, investigated Harding's Attorney General, Harry Daugherty, who resigned in March 1924. Out of that investigation came *McGrain v. Daugherty* (1927), in which the Supreme Court upheld the power of Congress to compel testimony. The subpoena power the new majority now holds was confirmed by Democrats doing careful work.

The same story shows how it goes wrong. A month after Daugherty resigned, a federal grand jury in Montana indicted Wheeler over his private law practice. Wheeler and his allies saw it as retaliation. The Senate examined the charges and overwhelmingly exonerated him, and a jury later acquitted him of all of them. A decade later, Roosevelt's case against Mellon showed the same weakness from the other side.

We have seen this situation before: a new majority with subpoena power, a base that wants someone punished, and a very rich, unpopular man who spent heavily against it. In 1933 that man was Andrew Mellon. Today it is Elon Musk. Roosevelt chose a flimsy charge that made headlines over real conduct that deserved scrutiny, and history has judged him poorly for it.

If Musk or anyone else is investigated, investigate what they actually did: the contracts, the conflicts, the conduct while advising the government. Do not build a case simply because he is rich or unpopular. One of the CNN panelists said to do it in good faith, "consistently across the board." Luria made the same point about the Armed Services Committee: when the only tough questions come when there's a president of the other party, "that's not oversight, that's just politics." And Wheeler, who exposed a Republican Attorney General in 1924, led the fight against his own party's President in 1937. That is the standard.

Post put the stakes plainly. Aristotle, he noted, defined democracy as taking turns, and this election was an example of it. But Post warned that if Democrats use the victory to demonize the opposition, they will put democracy further at risk.

## What David does next

Talarico told his election-night crowd the story of David and Goliath. "You were my slingshot," he said. "The people of Texas were my slingshot." In his telling, the Goliaths were "the richest man in the world, the most powerful man in the world, and the entire Washington DC establishment."

He earned the story. But the same speech also said that "the real fight in this country is not left versus right. It is top versus bottom," and described billionaires who "own the politicians." That is the language of Roosevelt's 1936 attacks on "economic royalists," and it leads toward the Mellon case. Talarico's own result argues against it. Musk spent heavily and lost. If billionaires owned the politicians, Talarico would not be a senator. His win is the best evidence that the system is sturdier than his speech allows. The better part of that speech is the open hand: "When you extend an open hand rather than a closed fist, you'll be surprised by who takes that hand." Clinton, in Florida, asked people to argue "with an outstretched hand, not a clenched fist." Two Democrats, a generation apart, reached for the same image.

So what does David do after Goliath? Scripture answers in detail. King Saul turns on him and hunts him. In the cave at En-gedi, David could kill Saul; he cuts off the corner of Saul's robe instead, and then regrets even that. Later he slips into Saul's camp at night, takes his spear and water jar, and leaves him alive. When Saul dies, David does not celebrate. He writes the lament "How are the mighty fallen." As king, he asks, "Is there yet any that is left of the house of Saul, that I may shew him kindness?" and brings Saul's lame grandson, Mephibosheth, to eat at his table for the rest of his life. And when David himself abuses his power, the prophet Nathan confronts him, "Thou art the man," and David repents rather than punishing the prophet.

That is a program for a new majority. Show restraint toward the powerful people you have beaten. Be generous to the other side's voters and donors, who are still your constituents. And accept oversight of your own side when it comes. Every David needs a Nathan.

## Build what works

Then deliver. Hubbard's advice is to aim at two things: growth, and "a full participation economy." "Don't be glib and say AI will make us better off," he says. Tell people how they will take part. On affordability, he names electricity, permitting reform, and the question of why it costs so much to build housing in particular places. "I don't think they're going to do any of that," he adds, "but I think that's the thing that needs to be done." The new majority can prove him wrong on the first half.

When asked whether that means a big government program, Hubbard answered with history. "I don't think it's a big government program. I think it's a big idea. Remember the land grant colleges were literally just land grants." The Morrill Act of 1862, a Republican measure signed by Abraham Lincoln, gave the states federal land to sell and told them to build colleges with the proceeds. It helped turn a farm economy into an industrial one. The GI Bill of 1944, signed by Roosevelt, sent a generation of veterans to college and into homes of their own. One came from Lincoln's party and one from Roosevelt's. Both were tried, both worked, and both trusted ordinary people to use an opportunity well.

That is where to look first: at programs that history has already tested.

## Read the historians, not just the summaries

This is my teachable point of view.

Be educated. Be informed. Do not blindly follow the summaries the news channels give us. The ideas we hold as citizens should be informed by what has actually been tried before, what worked and what did not. We have access to a hundred years of wonderful, interesting work by historians. Let's read it and listen to it, and compare what is happening now with what has happened before.

If we do, we can help these new leaders think through their policies, not just applaud them. We can help bring the national conversation back toward an educated and civil one. Hubbard asked both parties for "a message in the middle." The historians have given us most of it already.

Let's be smart. Let's think about governing this country. Let's recognize the capable people who have been elected, and let's consider a centrism that may represent intellectual humility. By that I mean not splitting the difference between the parties, but knowing that the economy and the government are complex, that history has already tested many of the ideas, and that a new majority's first duty is to learn from the results.

Let the people vote. Then let the people they elect do their reading.
