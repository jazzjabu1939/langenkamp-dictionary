# The Weather Is Not the Trade

*Rising food prices, strained farms, and the question of what comes next in 2027.*

*Originally published at [Freedom Tomato on Substack](https://freedomtomato.substack.com/p/the-weather-is-not-the-trade) on September 27, 2026.*

---

My earliest education in agricultural investment took place on a Massey Ferguson 135.

My father, Robert Dobie Langenkamp, drove it shirtless in the Oklahoma heat. I rode with him. Later, my sister Heather and I were allowed to drive it ourselves. We were learning to steer, not calculate a return on a farm investment.

The wind seemed always to be blowing.

Dad was a lawyer in Tulsa. He had studied at Stanford and Harvard, built a successful legal career, and developed a strong interest in land and cattle. Our family’s connection to Tulsa land went back further: the family history includes ownership of land beneath what is now Tulsa International Airport.

In the 1970s, after the speculative enthusiasm of the late sixties had given way to a much less agreeable stock market, he invested in ranch land. Roger Lowenstein’s Buffett: The Making of an American Capitalist brings that earlier investment world to mind. Dad was no Warren Buffett. But his attraction to something useful and tangible made sense to me, even if, as I sometimes joke, he was a few decades early.

He bought land near Inola and later near Colcord, in northeastern Oklahoma, not far from the northwestern Arkansas region around Bentonville. Law remained his main occupation. He was more than a hobby rancher, though not by much, and the rest of us were enlisted on weekends.

At local cattle auctions we bought Herefords and a bull named Brutus. My uncle Donald Hohmann, a Tulsa veterinarian, came to help vaccinate the cattle. At six feet five, he was a large presence and the kindest uncle I had. When I think of that ranch, I remember people and animals before I remember prices.

Dad told Lucy, my younger sister, that he had learned much of what he knew about raising cattle from a book published by Oklahoma State University. He also learned by watching other people. An education at Stanford and Harvard did not prevent him from finding something useful in an Oklahoma cattle book.

I offer this background to place myself, not to claim an occupation. I have worked around cattle and spent weekends on a ranch. I do not wear boots and a cowboy hat to work each day. There is a considerable distance between knowing something of that world and depending on it for a living.

## A sale I still think about



By the time his health began to fail, Dad had two ranches. At Colcord he had built a herd of just over a hundred cows, raising Black Angus from registered stock. He also had a Brangus bull—an Angus–Brahman cross. Brahman ancestry brings heat tolerance. This particular bull also brought a disposition that encouraged caution.

Failing health meant Dad had to sell the herd. In our conversations before he died, the sale stayed with me. I remember a price of about $1.80 a pound, though I am not sure of the weight basis or exactly which animals that figure covered. I would need the sale records before comparing it with prices today. A price for live cattle is not a retail price for beef.

Since his death, I have returned to that conversation more than once. I suspect many of us keep talking to the dead, conducting an imperfect sort of telepathy in which we must supply both sides.

Dad, if you could see the price of beef now, would you be kicking yourself for selling—or telling us you had always said cattle and land were a good investment?

Of course, he had not sold because he had lost an argument with the market. His health required it. An investment can outlast the investor’s ability to manage it. That is a lesson no later price increase can undo.

It is part of what I bring to the present question. Watching farm auctions and a recent video about El Niño, I started wondering whether several things were happening at once: hard weather, strained farm finances, disrupted trade, and food prices already climbing. Reports of large speculative positions in agricultural markets added another reason to look closely.

Could commodity prices spike in 2027? Should investment in the farm economy have a larger place in a diversified portfolio?

The questions deserve inspection. Family memory gives me a reason to ask them, not an answer.

## Prices have already moved



This is not simply a prediction that food commodities might become more expensive. Some already have.

The [FAO Food Price Index for August 2026](https://www.fao.org/worldfoodsituation/foodpricesindex/en/), released in September, rose 1.9 percent from July and stood 2.5 percent above its level a year earlier. International wheat prices rose 2.6 percent during August and were 15 percent higher than a year earlier. The sugar index jumped 11.9 percent in the month.

The comparison periods matter: a monthly jump is not an annual return, and an international food-price index is not the price of a supermarket basket or the performance of an investment fund.

The same report supplies some perspective. The overall food index remained 16.8 percent below its March 2022 peak. Dairy prices rose during August but were still substantially below their year-earlier level. Even within a rising category, individual products and producing regions moved differently.

Some commodities are rising. That’s an observation, and not yet an investment thesis. Wheat, sugar, oil, copper, and milk respond to different supplies and demands.

And if prices have already risen, the investor faces a less comfortable question: how much of the story has someone else already paid for?

## A farm can disappear without its fields disappearing



A retirement auction has an emotional clarity that an agricultural balance sheet lacks. An occupation ends. Machinery assembled over decades is dispersed. A family closes a chapter.

Yet the land may continue producing under a neighboring farmer or a new owner. Fewer farm businesses need not mean fewer acres planted or a smaller harvest. Consolidation can preserve output even while changing the character of rural life.

Financial distress can eventually reduce production: farmers may cut fertilizer use, postpone maintenance, lose access to operating credit, or leave land unplanted. But those are mechanisms to investigate, not consequences we can assume from a sale notice.

Nor should the human toll be reduced to a bullish signal. A suicide is not a data point that conveniently completes an investment argument. There is evidence of a historical rise: a [2023 study of male farmers in participating US states](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10336572/) found an estimated 2.4 percent annual increase in suicide rates over 2003–2017. That finding does not establish a new surge today, but it gives the concern a basis beyond anecdote.

The [USDA’s September farm-income forecast](https://www.ers.usda.gov/topics/farm-economy/farm-sector-income-finances/farm-sector-income-forecast) illustrates the complication. It projects US crop receipts rising 6.1 percent in 2026 while total farm production expenses rise 4.5 percent. Overall net farm income is forecast to fall 2.6 percent in nominal terms, with considerable differences among agricultural sectors.

Higher revenues and financial strain can coexist. What matters to a farmer is what remains after the bills are paid. What matters to the food supply is whether the next crop gets planted and brought in.

These are related questions, but they are not interchangeable. We could be facing scarcity.

## The auction yard tells a less uniform story



The equipment auctions we follow do not look like an indiscriminate collapse. Some machines still attract firm bids. I have also had the impression that equipment and supplies used in livestock farming have held up better than some machinery used to grow crops. Perhaps part of that impression reflects which sales have caught my attention.

There is some broader support for stabilization. A [September 17 report from Sandhills Global](https://www.tractorhouse.com/blog/sandhills-news/2026/09/used-farm-equipment-inventory-levels-continue-to-decline-as-auction-values-see-modest-increases), covering August, reported modest increases in auction values and declining equipment inventories. This is a report on its own markets, not a census of every farm sale.

Our notes from the September 26 MacFadden auction in Sharon Springs, New York, offer concrete examples. Reported sale prices included $49,000 for a John Deere 5075E advertised with 157 hours and a loader package, $26,000 for a Kubota L3560 with 501 hours, cab and loader, and $165,000 for a 2023 John Deere 6R 215 advertised with 253 hours. These are recorded sale results, not dealer asking prices; our notes do not establish the additional fees or the buyers’ total costs. By “real farm tractors” here, I mean machines with more than 100 engine horsepower—a working distinction for this discussion, not a claim that smaller tractors do no real farming. Of these three, the John Deere 6R 215 belongs in that larger category; the 5075E and Kubota L3560 do not. Their prices should not be treated as one market.

Those figures show that buyers were willing to commit money to selected machines. They do not, by themselves, show that prices were steady. For that we need earlier sales of comparable machines: similar age, hours, condition, attachments, and location. A sale full of unusually good tractors can produce a rising average in a falling market.

Still, these observations complicate the picture of farms liquidating equipment into a market with no buyers. They suggest a question worth pursuing: has part of the used-equipment market begun to find a floor? Are people thinking about planting corn and beans again with some confidence of making money? I mean renewing investment or expanding acreage, not that farmers as a whole stopped planting.

By a floor, I mean sustained stability in comparable sale prices—not one lively auction, and not prices returning to their former peaks.

## Brazil is part of the price on an American farm



The pressure on American corn and soybean growers is not just a story about American weather. Brazil has changed the market. A [USDA review published in 2022](https://www.ers.usda.gov/amber-waves/2022/september/brazil-s-momentum-as-a-global-agricultural-supplier-faces-headwinds) described agricultural output doubling over 2000–20. Brazilian soybean exports, only 40 percent of US exports in 2000, had overtaken them by the time of that review.

This was not simply a matter of planting more land. Agricultural research, notably through Embrapa, helped adapt crops to tropical conditions. Cultivation spread into the Cerrado, yields improved, and farmers could follow soybeans with a second crop of corn. Strong Chinese demand gave the additional beans a buyer. Better transport made it easier to get them to port.

The cost difference matters. [USDA researchers](https://www.ers.usda.gov/data-products/charts-of-note/109142) found that Brazilian soybean production costs per acre averaged 22.5 percent below US costs over 2010/11–2021/22, largely because of lower land and capital costs. They also found that Brazil’s average inland transport cost per metric ton fell 21.4 percent between the periods 2008/09–2012/13 and 2017/18–2021/22. Those are historical cost comparisons, not today’s export quotations, but they help explain the competitive shift.

More exportable soybeans, supplied competitively, put downward pressure on world prices relative to what they would otherwise be. American growers must compete for buyers who can turn to Brazil. That pressure can reach the price offered at an American grain elevator, even when the local farmer’s costs have not fallen. Exchange rates, freight, tariffs, and demand from China affect which origin is cheapest for a particular shipment; Brazilian beans are not always cheaper.

For corn and beans, the question is whether the expected selling price will cover the next season’s costs. A [February 2026 comparison of representative farms in Iowa and Mato Grosso](https://farmdocdaily.illinois.edu/2026/02/us-and-brazil-soybean-competitiveness-farm-level-costs-and-returns.html) found soybean losses on the Iowa farm in 2024 while the Brazilian farm remained profitable. These were two representative operations, not national averages. Still, they show why “low prices” is often shorthand for prices too low for a particular farm’s costs.

Nor should I freeze that picture in time. The [USDA soybean outlook available in September 2026](https://www.ers.usda.gov/topics/crops/soybeans-and-oil-crops/market-outlook) raised its 2026/27 US season-average price forecast to $12 a bushel. That is a forecast, not a local cash bid or a guarantee of profit. Brazil’s expansion remains a competitive pressure even when prices recover.

This cuts both ways for 2027. Another strong Brazilian harvest could restrain a rally and keep American margins tight. A serious production shortfall in Brazil would matter precisely because the world has come to rely on its output. The country that has helped hold prices down could also be where a supply shock begins.

## Livestock and crops need separate accounts



My impression of strength around beef, lamb, and goats suggests another possibility. Better livestock returns could support demand for hay equipment, feeders, fencing, handling equipment, and trailers even while crop growers defer large machinery purchases.

There is evidence for part of this argument. USDA’s September forecast expects US cattle and calf receipts to rise 5.2 percent in 2026 as cattle prices continue to increase. FAO reported firmer international sheep-meat prices in August, but falling international bovine-meat quotations. Different markets can move in opposite directions. Neither report establishes a broad rise in goat prices or proves that livestock-equipment prices are strengthening.

For now, the livestock-equipment connection is a hypothesis drawn from observation, not a measured national trend. I’d want to see actual sales of livestock and hay equipment held up against crop machinery over several months before trusting it.

Even strong animal prices do not settle the matter. A breeder selling calves, a feedlot buying them, and a farmer buying breeding stock face different costs. Expensive animals can raise one operator’s income and another’s working-capital needs. Higher grain prices can squeeze feeding margins. A smaller herd can command higher prices while requiring less equipment and feed in total.

The useful question is not simply whether beef is expensive. It is which producers in which regions (or countries) have money left to invest, and what they are buying.

## The land is valuable, but not invulnerable



Dad saw something intrinsically valuable in land. It could support animals and produce food. But productive capacity is something to maintain, not merely possess.

The [Dust Bowl](https://www.britannica.com/place/Dust-Bowl) is a warning against forgetting that distinction. Across parts of the Great Plains, cultivation removed native grass cover and left soil exposed. Severe drought, failed crops, and strong winds in the 1930s turned that vulnerability into disaster. The problem was not simply that wheat had shallower roots. It was the loss of protective vegetation and soil structure, combined with farming practices poorly suited to prolonged drought.

Our ranches in northeastern Oklahoma were not in the Dust Bowl’s hardest-hit southern High Plains. But the larger lesson belongs to anyone who treats land as a durable asset: its value depends on living systems that can be damaged.

I do not know whether we face ecological breakdowns of comparable severity in the years ahead. The [El Niño video I watched on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KxNHlkNuqG0) made me think harder about the risk of extreme heat in 2027. We would be foolish to ignore this history. The plains remain vital to the grains and livestock that supply processors, food companies, and our tables.

This is one reason I enjoy [Patrick Shivers’ farming videos](https://www.youtube.com/@PatrickShivers). A farmer in south Georgia, he shows machinery, field work, and trials, including comparisons of tilled and no-till ground. I appreciate seeing the decisions and work behind the finished crop. No-till is not a universal cure, but questions about retaining soil and moisture belong in an investment discussion as much as questions about prices.

[NOAA’s September 10 outlook](https://www.cpc.ncep.noaa.gov/products/analysis_monitoring/enso_disc_sep2026/ensodisc.shtml) assigns a greater than 90 percent probability to a very strong El Niño during the Northern Hemisphere autumn and winter of 2026–27. It also explicitly says that associated impacts are not guaranteed. Predicting how that Pacific warming will affect rainfall in a particular growing region is harder than identifying the ocean warming itself.

El Niño is not a weather forecast for every field. Rain arriving in one region can relieve a drought; too much rain elsewhere can interrupt planting or harvesting. The same weather anomaly can have different consequences depending on the crop and its stage of growth.

For prices, we need another step. Can production elsewhere compensate? How much grain is in storage, and how much is actually available for export? Will governments restrict shipments? Will buyers substitute one feed grain for another? The prospect of drought in the Amazon makes me take notice, especially given Brazil’s importance to world food supplies. But the Amazon basin is not a map of Brazil’s soybean acreage. Much of that production is in the Cerrado and other growing regions. For the investment question, I want to know whether heat or poor rainfall will reach those fields during planting and crop development.

A disappointing harvest matters more when inventories are thin. In the short run, people cannot stop eating and farmers cannot produce an extra harvest on demand. A modest shortfall in available supply can therefore cause a much larger price increase. That is a plausible route to a commodity-price spike in 2027—not proof that one will occur.

## A harvest still has to reach its buyer



Weather is only part of the risk. War can interrupt the supply of grain without destroying the crop.

Russian attacks on Ukraine’s grain infrastructure threaten the ports through which harvests reach foreign buyers. An [August 13 report from Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/video/newsfeed/2026/8/13/13-08-sv-ukraine-russia-grain-attack-ur) described strikes on grain infrastructure in the Odesa region, alongside Ukrainian attacks on Russian grain terminals at Novorossiysk. Damage to loading facilities, disrupted sailings, and higher insurance costs can reduce the grain available to importers or raise the cost of delivering it.

The price effects need not be uniform. Grain trapped behind a damaged export route can fetch less at the farm while costing more at its destination. A global price rise is not necessarily a windfall for the farmer whose route to market has been cut.

Ukraine’s strikes on Russian oil refineries create a second pressure. [CNBC reported on September 14](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/14/trump-ukraine-russia-diesel-prices-oil.html) that the attacks had disrupted refining and that Russia had extended restrictions on diesel exports. Reduced supplies of refined fuel can tighten diesel markets even when crude oil remains available. The report also identified Middle Eastern supply disruptions and rising crude prices: the refinery attacks contribute to the pressure, but do not explain every increase at the pump.

Tractors run on diesel. So do many combines, irrigation pumps, and the trucks that carry seed, fertilizer, animals, and harvested crops. More expensive diesel raises the cost of producing food and moving it. It does not guarantee that farmers can charge enough to recover the increase. Some may instead face narrower margins or reduce spending on the next crop.

This gives the 2027 hypothesis a geopolitical dimension. I am refraining from using the phrase “perfect storm.” Poor harvests, damaged export routes, and expensive fuel could reinforce one another. Repairs, restored shipping, or an easing of conflict could relieve the pressure. I want to watch actual export volumes, refinery output, and delivered fuel costs—not just count attacks.

## The funds have noticed



The investment community has not been waiting for my afternoon viewing habits.

[Saxo’s September commentary on trader positioning](https://www.home.saxo/en-mena/content/articles/commodities/cot-on-forex-and-commodities---week-to-15-september-2026-21092026) described agricultural speculative positions as historically elevated, even as some profits were being taken. This is evidence that the theme has attracted attention, not proof that the funds know what the next harvest will bring. We don’t promise to be in the info loop with regard to commodity trading.

Positions are also not the same as fresh dollars flowing into a market. A change in a net position can reflect new buying, the closing of bets against prices, or both.

For a prospective investor, crowded optimism cuts two ways. Further buying can support a rally. Disappointing news can also send many holders toward the exit together.

Being right about a difficult year for agriculture does not guarantee being right about a purchase made after prices have anticipated it.

## Could prices spike in 2027?



Yes. The question is which prices, for how long, and how much of that risk is already reflected in today’s market.

One possibility is a more persistent squeeze. Weather damages crops in several important exporting regions, attacks interrupt grain shipments and fuel supplies, and governments restrict exports to protect domestic supplies. Inventories decline while production and transport costs rise. Demand proves difficult to reduce. In that setting, already rising prices could have further to go.

A second possibility is a patchwork year. Some crops suffer, others recover, and strong harvests elsewhere offset part of the damage. Prices spike around particular shortages without producing a general agricultural boom. This strikes me as a more useful starting assumption than expecting every commodity to rise together.

A third possibility is that the shortage story runs ahead of the shortage. Weather proves less damaging than feared, damaged facilities return to service, and shipping disruptions ease. Farmers respond to higher prices with additional planting. Buyers economize or substitute. Speculative positions unwind, and prices fall despite continuing hardship in parts of the farming economy.

These are scenarios, not assigned probabilities. Nor is a brief price spike the same as a high annual average. A prediction about 2027 should say which outcome it means before claiming success afterward.

## Buying the story is not the same as buying a farm



There are several ways to invest in this area, and they express different beliefs.

A commodity fund offers exposure to a basket or an individual crop, commonly through futures contracts. It does not necessarily deliver the return implied by a headline about spot prices: fees and the cost of replacing expiring contracts matter.

An agricultural company adds another business between the investor and the crop. Fertilizer producers depend on their own selling prices and input costs. Equipment makers need customers willing and able to buy machinery. High food prices do not automatically make either a good investment.

Farmland is different again. Its economics involve purchase price, rent, soil, water, financing, and the ability of a tenant or operator to earn a living. A drought can make grain more valuable while making a particular farm less profitable.

An equipment recovery and a commodity-price spike are also different investment cases. Used machines might hold their value because fewer are offered for sale or because new replacements are expensive. Neither explanation requires a food shortage. Conversely, a crop failure might raise grain prices while leaving affected farmers unable to buy machinery.

We can be right about the pressure and still buy the wrong investment, at the wrong price, for the wrong length of time.

## What I want to watch



I have asked Thea, my AI assistant, to help me track crop prospects, stocks, farm costs, Black Sea exports, diesel prices, and trader positioning week to week. Alongside those measures, I want to compare equipment sale results by category. The purpose is to test an idea, not produce a fresh reason to buy every Saturday.

The investment hypothesis deserves more careful study. It would strengthen if exportable stocks fell, crop forecasts worsened across several suppliers, and demand held up. It would weaken if harvests replenished stocks or buyers cut consumption. Equipment stabilization would be a separate finding, useful for assessing farm spending but insufficient to predict grain prices.

Before taking a position, I would want to name the commodity, the expected catalyst, the holding period, and the loss I could accept. I would also want to explain why the investment remains attractive after the price increases already recorded. A sound account of the world is only the beginning of a sound purchase.

For now, my view is that the possibility of further agricultural price increases deserves attention. The evidence does not justify treating all commodities as one winning trade.

There is a wider question here too. A system can maintain its total output while placing extraordinary strain on the people producing it. A successful harvest does not settle whether those arrangements are durable or humane. Equally, visible hardship does not prove that a global shortage is imminent.

We should care whether farmers can continue farming and whether households can afford to eat. This is about morality. We can also ask how to invest intelligently in a changing world, supporting farmers and feeding the citizenry. Taking the first question seriously helps with the second question.

I do not have a crystal ball. Still, this may be a useful time to ask what role the farm economy could play in a diversified portfolio. Diversification is a possibility to examine, not a property every agricultural investment possesses.

There is also a distinction between investing in agricultural prices and helping agriculture. Buying a futures-based fund does not directly finance a farmer’s next crop. Providing capital for a viable farm business, equipment, or sound stewardship of land is a different undertaking, with its own risks and obligations.

I think Dad understood the appeal of owning something useful. What I learned from him includes the work, the animals, the wind, and the fact that a sale sometimes comes when life requires it rather than when the market recommends it.

If I could ask him about today’s prices, I would. He would probably tell me not to bother predicting them—any more than the price of oil. He had served in the US Department of Energy and run an Oklahoma oil company with my uncle. My grandfather wrote the original Langenkamp oilfield terminology dictionary. Oil was part of our family’s working life, so I can imagine the advice coming with some experience behind it. In his absence, I can at least try to bring the same curiosity to the question—and keep learning from the people who do the work.
